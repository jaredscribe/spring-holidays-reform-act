# Spring Holidays Reform Act

A proposal to restructure the spring commemorative calendar: establish **May 8**
as a federal holiday, **repeal Memorial Day**, and give **Juneteenth National
Independence Day** the statutory observance section it has never had.

**Status: draft, not published as a recommendation. Read the objections first.**

## Read this before anything else

This is the most contested proposal in this project, and unlike its companion
drafts it does not merely add something — it **abolishes a federal holiday**.
Research conducted 2026-09-04 against primary sources establishes two things
the drafter should weigh before proceeding:

**1. The premise, as originally stated, is not supported by law.** The proposal
began from the idea that Memorial Day is a Civil-War observance whose function
could be relocated to Juneteenth. **No operative federal statute says that.**
[36 U.S.C. §
116](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title36-section116&num=0&edition=prelim)
designates the day and asks the President to proclaim a period of prayer for
peace — it names no war, no dead, and no veterans. The only statement of
purpose Congress has ever enacted is the [National Moment of Remembrance Act
(Pub. L.
106–579)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-106publ579/html/PLAW-106publ579.htm)
§ 2, and it is explicitly all-wars: tribute to those "who have made the
ultimate sacrifice **in service to the United States**." Memorial Day's
*origin* is the Civil War. Its *present function*, broadened by custom after
the First World War, is the dead of every American war — and no statute ever
effected or defined that broadening.

**2. There is no precedent for what this bill does.** Congress has never
removed a day from [5 U.S.C. §
6103](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title5-section6103&num=0&edition=prelim)
or its predecessors. The nearest attempt is instructive: in July 2020 two
senators proposed swapping **Columbus Day** — the holiday they expressly chose
as least defended — for **Juneteenth**. It failed within days, was withdrawn,
and Juneteenth was enacted a year later as an eleventh holiday with no offset
at all.

The full case against is set out in
[the objections section](spring-holidays-reform-act.md#objections-and-how-this-draft-answers-them)
of the bill, stated as an opponent would state it rather than softened.

## What the bill actually proposes

Not the abolition of a memorial — its **division between two anchored days**:

| | receives | anchored to |
|---|---|---|
| **Juneteenth**, June 19 | the Union dead of the Civil War | the completion of emancipation, the purpose they died for |
| **Victory Day**, May 8 | the dead of every American war | a victory won |

Memorial Day is presently joined to no event at all. Its ceremonies seldom
mention the war it came from, and it has drifted toward observance without
recollection. Each memorial here gains what a general day of mourning lacks —
**an account of what the dead accomplished.**

The obvious retort is that this anchors mourning for all wars to one war's
victory. Congress has already done precisely that and it was uncontroversial:
**Armistice Day**, established to mark one specific event, became **Veterans
Day** in 1954, honoring the veterans of every war while staying fixed to the
1918 armistice date. Seventy years on, nobody finds it strange.

## Two drafts, deliberately

| | Approach | Abolishes anything? |
|---|---|---|
| **[Title I](spring-holidays-reform-act.md)** | As directed: establish May 8, repeal Memorial Day, create a Juneteenth observance section that receives the Civil-War commemoration | Yes — Memorial Day |
| **[The alternative](spring-holidays-reform-act.md#the-alternative-the-1954-template)** | Amend § 116 to give Memorial Day the scope language it lacks, recognizing its emancipationist origins; establish May 8; give Juneteenth its own § 119 | No |

The alternative follows a precedent directly on point. In 1954 Congress
converted **Armistice Day** — a holiday commemorating one specific event, the
1918 armistice — into **Veterans Day**, honoring the veterans of all wars, by a
statute that struck one word and inserted another ([Act of June 1, 1954, ch.
250, 68 Stat.
168](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-68/pdf/STATUTE-68-Pg168.pdf)).
Nothing was abolished and no constituency was displaced. It passed without
controversy.

If the drafter's underlying goal is to reconnect national commemoration to
emancipation and to the Union dead, the alternative accomplishes it, creates
the scope language § 116 currently lacks, gives Juneteenth a designation
section it does not have, and does not ask Congress to do something it has
never once done.
